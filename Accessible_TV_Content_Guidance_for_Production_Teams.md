# Accessible TV Content: Guidance for Production Teams

## Article overview

Accessible television enables more people to perceive, understand and enjoy content, including people who are Blind or partially sighted, Deaf, deaf, deafened or hard of hearing, Deafblind, neurodivergent, cognitively disabled or affected by situational and temporary barriers.

Accessibility should be considered from development and commissioning through production, post-production, delivery, distribution and archive. Adding access services at the end may be necessary, but it is rarely the most effective or creative approach.

This guide covers:

- subtitles and captions;
- audio description;
- on-screen text and graphics;
- sign-language access;
- dialogue intelligibility;
- cognitive access and photosensitivity;
- production workflow and responsibilities;
- testing with disabled audiences;
- asset, metadata and platform requirements; and
- inclusive creative practice.

## Scope and limits

This is practical production guidance, not a universal legal or delivery specification. Regulatory duties and access-service quotas usually apply to broadcasters or regulated services, while production companies may have contractual delivery obligations. The responsible team must confirm:

- commissioning requirements;
- the relevant territory and regulator;
- broadcaster and platform specifications;
- linear, on-demand, social and international versions;
- live and pre-recorded workflows;
- language and localisation requirements;
- archive and redelivery requirements; and
- who owns creation, quality assurance, metadata and publication of each access service.

In the United Kingdom, Ofcom's television access-services framework covers subtitling, audio description and signing for applicable broadcasters. On-demand and international services may be governed by different rules and specifications.

## 1. Subtitles and captions for Deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences

Text access should convey the speech and non-speech audio needed to understand the programme. It should be planned for live and pre-recorded content across every intended platform.

### Terminology

Terminology varies by country and platform:

- **Subtitles for Deaf and hard-of-hearing audiences**, often shortened to SDH, normally include dialogue, relevant sound, music information and speaker identification.
- **Closed captions** are captions the viewer can turn on or off. In some regions, this term covers much of the same function as SDH.
- **Open captions** or **burned-in subtitles** are permanently visible in the picture.
- **Language subtitles** primarily translate or transcribe dialogue for viewers who can hear the soundtrack and may omit important non-speech audio.

The production specification should define the intended audience and required asset. Do not assume that a language-subtitle file provides equivalent access for Deaf audiences.

### Editorial requirements

High-quality subtitles or captions should:

- convey the meaning, tone and relevant wording of speech accurately;
- identify speakers when the identity is not visually obvious;
- identify significant sounds, off-screen events, music and meaningful silence;
- distinguish lyrics from dialogue where required by the house style;
- preserve humour, wordplay, emphasis and relevant vocal information where practicable;
- avoid censoring, simplifying or paraphrasing disabled audiences' access unless editorial or reading-speed constraints require it; and
- follow the spelling, punctuation, terminology and language conventions specified by the commissioner or platform.

Verbatim transcription is not always possible or desirable. Editing may be needed for reading speed, timing or clarity, particularly in dense or overlapping speech. Any editing should preserve meaning and be governed by an approved style guide.

### Timing and synchronisation

Subtitles should:

- appear in synchrony with speech and relevant sounds;
- remain long enough to be read without creating misleading delay;
- avoid revealing dialogue or plot events prematurely;
- handle shot changes according to the delivery specification;
- identify overlapping speakers clearly; and
- be reviewed in the final programme context, not only as a text file.

### Presentation

The delivery format determines how much appearance can be controlled by the production team. Closed-caption styling may be rendered by the platform or viewer's settings; open captions are part of the picture.

Where presentation is controlled, ensure:

- sufficient contrast against changing images;
- legible type and size at expected viewing distances and screen sizes;
- line length and line breaks that support comprehension;
- placement that does not hide faces, signers, names, essential action or graphics;
- safe positioning across intended aspect ratios and cropped versions; and
- appropriate colour use where colour identifies speakers.

### Live subtitles

Live subtitles require trained operators and defined latency, accuracy and correction procedures. Workflows may use respeaking, stenography, automatic speech recognition or combinations of these methods.

Automatic speech recognition alone should not be assumed to provide broadcast-quality access. Names, specialist vocabulary, accents, rapid speech, sound quality and breaking news can produce serious errors. Prepare scripts, running orders, contributor names and terminology in advance, and provide human supervision or intervention appropriate to the risk.

Monitor:

- accuracy;
- latency;
- speaker identification;
- completeness;
- placement;
- recovery after errors or technical failure; and
- the effect of delay on comedy, sport, news and audience participation.

## 2. Audio description for Blind and partially sighted audiences

Audio description is an additional narrative track that communicates visual information needed to follow or appreciate the programme.

### What to describe

Depending on context, description may cover:

- actions and physical relationships;
- changes of location or time;
- characters and new arrivals;
- facial expressions, gestures and body language;
- clothing, objects and visual detail relevant to story or tone;
- on-screen text, captions, messages, credits and graphics;
- visual jokes, montage, dance and action sequences; and
- information communicated only through colour, framing, editing or visual symbolism.

Not every visible detail needs to be described. Prioritise information that supports comprehension, character, atmosphere and the creative intention.

### Writing approach

The original article advised using “clear and neutral language, avoiding interpretation or emotion.” That is too absolute.

Description should be accurate and should not claim access to a character's thoughts. However, it may describe visibly evident emotion, performance, style and atmosphere. The vocabulary, rhythm and narrator performance should fit the genre and programme without competing with it.

Good description is:

- concise enough to fit the available space;
- specific rather than vague;
- faithful to what the programme communicates visually;
- sensitive to plot reveals and authorial intention;
- consistent in naming people, places and objects; and
- written for listening, not as prose intended to be read silently.

### Timing and mix

Descriptions are normally placed in pauses where they do not obscure essential dialogue or significant sound. This can be difficult in programmes with continuous speech or sound.

Production teams can improve the result by:

- involving the audio-description team early;
- sharing scripts, locked pictures, continuity information and pronunciation guidance;
- identifying visually dense or silent sequences before the final mix;
- retaining suitable dialogue, music and effects stems;
- allowing space for integrated description where creatively appropriate; and
- checking the final AD mix for balance, intelligibility and synchronisation.

Where pauses are insufficient, commissioners and platforms may support alternative approaches such as extended description or an integrated described version. Confirm the required format before production.

## 3. On-screen text, captions and graphics

Information conveyed visually must be considered both within the original programme and within access services.

### Visual presentation

Where the production controls the graphic design:

- use legible type at the expected viewing distance;
- provide sufficient contrast and avoid relying on colour alone;
- allow enough time to read the content;
- avoid unnecessary movement, distortion or visual clutter;
- keep essential text within the relevant safe area;
- test on small screens and compressed versions; and
- check how subtitles, signers, logos and platform overlays interact with the composition.

### Equivalent access

Important names, locations, dates, messages, charts and other text should be available through the soundtrack, audio description or another accessible alternative where needed.

Do not automatically voice every graphic. Repeating visible and spoken information can become intrusive. Decide what information is essential and coordinate the main narration, dialogue, audio description and subtitles.

Complex data visualisations may require an accessible explanation or descriptive transcript in addition to audio description.

## 4. Sign-language access

Some audiences use a sign language as their first or preferred language. Sign-language access may be appropriate or required for news, public information, education, children's content and other programming.

### Terminology and language

Sign languages are complete natural languages. They are not universal. British Sign Language, American Sign Language and Auslan are distinct languages, and regional and community variation exists within them.

Define the target audience, language and territory before commissioning access.

Distinguish between:

- an interpreter translating spoken content;
- a Deaf translator working between written or spoken content and a sign language;
- an in-vision signer added to an existing programme; and
- sign-presented content created directly in sign language.

### Framing and presentation

Where an in-vision signer or interpreter is used:

- provide sufficient image size to perceive hands, face, body and non-manual features;
- maintain consistent and suitable framing;
- use an uncluttered, contrasting background;
- avoid covering the signer with captions, tickers, graphics or platform controls;
- preserve the intended framing in alternative aspect ratios and cropped versions;
- coordinate pace and editorial preparation; and
- include Deaf sign-language users in quality assurance.

Signing should be planned as a language service, not treated as a decorative overlay.

## 5. Dialogue intelligibility and sound design

Clear sound benefits many audiences, including people with hearing loss, auditory-processing differences, cognitive disabilities, language learners and people listening in difficult environments.

Production teams should:

- record clean dialogue and appropriate room tone;
- control avoidable masking of speech by music and effects;
- manage overlapping dialogue deliberately;
- retain clean dialogue and separate music-and-effects assets where required;
- check downmixes, loudness, dynamics and device variation;
- review intelligibility on ordinary televisions, mobile devices and headphones; and
- avoid treating subtitles as a remedy for an unnecessarily unintelligible main mix.

This does not require flat or uncreative sound. It requires deliberate choices and evidence that important speech and cues remain available to the intended audience.

## 6. Cognitive access, motion and photosensitivity

The original guidance focused mainly on sensory access. Inclusive production should also consider cognitive, neurological and seizure-related barriers.

### Cognitive access

Depending on genre and purpose, consider:

- clear narrative signposting;
- consistent names and terminology;
- readable graphics and information hierarchy;
- enough time to process essential information;
- avoiding unnecessary simultaneous demands across speech, text and imagery;
- explaining unfamiliar processes or instructions; and
- summaries, chapters or transcripts for complex factual content.

These practices should support rather than flatten creative complexity. Drama does not have to become simple; essential information should not be obscured accidentally.

### Flashing and intense motion

Identify flashing, rapid patterns and intense motion early enough for editorial or technical remediation. Apply the current broadcaster or platform test method and warning policy. A subjective internal review is not an adequate photosensitivity test.

## 7. Production workflow and responsibility

Accessibility should be an owned production workstream rather than an unfunded final delivery task.

| Stage | Required actions | Evidence to retain |
|---|---|---|
| Development and commissioning | Define audiences, access services, territories, platforms, budget, schedule and accountable owner | Accessibility brief, commissioner specification, responsibility map |
| Pre-production | Review scripts, graphics, planned text, sign-language needs, descriptive opportunities and photosensitivity risks | Script notes, design decisions, access-service plan |
| Production | Capture intelligible sound, clean dialogue, appropriate stems, visual references and accurate contributor information | Sound reports, continuity records, names and pronunciation list |
| Picture and sound post-production | Coordinate edit, graphics, mix and access-service preparation; avoid removing the space or assets required for access | Locked picture, scripts, spotting lists, audio stems, graphics inventory |
| Access-service production | Create, time, mix and review subtitles, AD, signing and transcripts with qualified practitioners | Versioned access-service assets and QA reports |
| Mastering and delivery | Package correct assets, identifiers, languages, metadata, frame rates and versions | Delivery manifest, automated validation and receipt |
| Platform publication | Verify discoverability, labels, playback, synchronisation and persistence after transcoding | Published-service QA and issue log |
| Archive and redelivery | Retain masters, access assets, metadata, rights and provenance for future formats | Archive inventory and version history |

The commissioner, producer, post-production facility, access-service provider, broadcaster and platform should agree who is responsible for each step. “The supplier handles it” is not a governance model.

## 8. Testing and review

Testing should combine specialist review, technical validation and disabled audience experience.

### Specialist quality assurance

Review each access service for:

- editorial accuracy;
- completeness;
- timing and synchronisation;
- language and terminology;
- presentation and collision with graphics;
- audio mix and intelligibility;
- metadata and version matching; and
- compliance with the delivery specification.

### Disabled audience evaluation

Include people who use the relevant access service. Test the final experience, not only the source asset.

Research may reveal:

- subtitles hiding key action or being difficult to follow;
- missing speaker or sound information;
- live-caption delay that changes meaning;
- audio description omitting essential action or overwhelming dialogue;
- poor AD mix balance;
- a signer too small to understand;
- inaccessible menus or undiscoverable access controls;
- confusing labels for available versions; and
- access assets lost or desynchronised after platform processing.

Internal review can find obvious defects but is not a substitute for qualified access-service review or disabled users' experience.

## 9. Platform, format and metadata requirements

Every intended destination may have different specifications. Obtain them before access-service production begins.

Confirm:

- accepted subtitle and caption formats;
- frame rate, time base and timecode convention;
- live and file-based workflows;
- character encoding and language codes;
- speaker, sound, music and positioning syntax;
- AD track layout, routing, loudness and mix requirements;
- signed-version picture format;
- transcript and descriptive-transcript requirements;
- programme, episode and asset identifiers;
- language, accessibility and role metadata;
- file naming and package structure;
- encryption or rights restrictions;
- validation and rejection rules; and
- archive and redelivery expectations.

Metadata is part of accessibility. A correct AD track or subtitle file that is mislabelled, undiscoverable or attached to the wrong programme is not an accessible outcome.

### Playback responsibility

Accessible playback controls and user interfaces are usually controlled by broadcasters and platforms rather than programme producers. Production teams should nevertheless:

- deliver the correct assets and metadata;
- state intended labels and languages;
- test published playback where access is available;
- report platform defects; and
- avoid assuming that successful file delivery means successful audience access.

## 10. Inclusive creative practice

Accessibility can enrich storytelling rather than merely describe it afterwards.

Production teams can:

- use sound design to communicate place, movement and narrative information;
- include important on-screen information naturally in dialogue or narration;
- build pauses or editorial space that support description without weakening the work;
- integrate sign language as part of character, performance or programme language;
- use accessible graphics and information design from the outset;
- consider integrated description and creative captions where the format allows;
- involve disabled writers, performers, directors, editors, access practitioners and consultants; and
- provide accessible working practices behind the camera as well as accessible output.

Creative access still needs a functional check. A visually inventive caption or unusual AD treatment is not successful if the intended audience cannot use it.

## Minimum pre-delivery checklist

- [ ] Access-service obligations and target audiences are documented.
- [ ] Commissioner, broadcaster and platform specifications are current.
- [ ] Required subtitle, AD, signed and transcript versions are complete.
- [ ] Names, terminology, language and pronunciation have been verified.
- [ ] Timing, synchronisation, mix and presentation have passed specialist QA.
- [ ] Graphics do not collide with subtitles or signers.
- [ ] Flashing and motion have been assessed using the required method.
- [ ] Every asset has the correct programme ID, language, role and accessibility metadata.
- [ ] Published playback or a representative end-to-end path has been tested.
- [ ] Disabled audience feedback has been considered where proportionate.
- [ ] Known limitations, waivers and corrective actions are recorded.
- [ ] Masters, access assets, rights and provenance are archived together.

## Summary

Accessible television is not only about meeting quotas or delivery specifications. It is about respecting audiences and ensuring that more people can share in the stories, information and culture television provides.

The practical instruction is simple:

> **Plan early. Assign responsibility. Collaborate with specialists and disabled people. Test the audience experience. Deliver and preserve every access asset with care.**

## A note on BBC history

An earlier diagnostic edition of this document incorrectly treated the source article's reference to BBC accessibility work in the 1950s as unsupported. That conclusion was too narrow: it considered the later histories of broadcast subtitling and audio description but overlooked the BBC's earlier work with signed and visually designed programmes for Deaf audiences.

The terminology used in historical records is inconsistent, and early output does not map neatly onto today's distinction between sign-interpreted and sign-presented programmes. The evidence nevertheless establishes a substantially earlier history:

- *For Deaf Children* began in 1952–53. The monthly BBC programme was created specifically for Deaf children and incorporated captions, mime and signing as its format developed. It is an early example of British television designed for a Deaf audience and predates *See Hear* by almost three decades;
- during the 1950s the BBC experimented with both interpreted and directly presented signed content for Deaf audiences, although surviving catalogues and later histories do not always label the two forms consistently;
- *Vision On* began in 1964 as the successor to *For Deaf Children*. It was designed for Deaf children, used signing and mime, and adopted a strongly visual, largely speech-free format that also attracted a wider children's audience;
- the first BBC teletext subtitles in 1979;
- regular live subtitling from 1986;
- participation in the European Commission-supported AUDETEL audio-description project during the 1990s;
- BBC television audio-description services from 2000; and
- the continuing development of signed programming, including *See Hear*, which began in 1981 and later became fully BSL-presented.

The BBC's accessibility history also reaches back into radio. The British Wireless for the Blind Fund—renamed Visably in 2026—was established in 1928 by Sir Ernest Beachcroft Beckwith Towse. The BBC supported its early development and fundraising: contemporary BBC material records BBC representation on the fund's committee, and the BBC broadcast the fund's first national fundraising appeal, delivered by Winston Churchill on Christmas Day 1929. The appeal helped fund the distribution of adapted wireless sets to Blind people. The founding role should therefore be attributed to Towse, while the BBC's organisational and broadcasting support should also be recognised.

These developments involved Deaf and Blind people, programme-makers, charities, campaigners, engineers, regulators, other broadcasters and access-service professionals. The history should acknowledge the BBC's pioneering contribution without erasing the people and partner organisations that shaped, funded, tested and used the services.
